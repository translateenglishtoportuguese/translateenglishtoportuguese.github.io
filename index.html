Getting Started

Portuguese for Beginners: A Friendly Starting Guide

Portuguese is spoken by more than 260 million people, and the great majority of them — over 210 million — live in Brazil. It is the language of Rio's beaches, São Paulo's boardrooms, Lisbon's old streets and a huge, warm diaspora. For an English speaker it is one of the most approachable big languages in the world: it uses the same alphabet, shares thousands of similar words, and Brazilians are famously patient and delighted when a visitor tries their language.

A language with music in it

Brazilian Portuguese sounds soft and melodic because unstressed vowels relax and many syllables glide together. You will hear the famous nasal sounds — words ending in -ão like pão (bread) hum through the nose — and the gentle "jee" sound where other Portuguese speakers say a hard "dee": bom dia (good morning) sounds like "bong JEE-ah" in most of Brazil. Do not aim for perfection on day one; a cheerful attempt earns smiles everywhere.

How Portuguese sentences are built

The word order will feel familiar: Portuguese is subject–verb–object, just like English. "I eat rice" becomes Eu como arroz — word for word. The real differences are smaller ones: adjectives usually come after the noun (uma casa grande — "a house big"), every noun is masculine or feminine, and verbs change their endings for each person. None of this blocks basic communication; you can be understood with simple sentences from your first week.

The first words to learn

Start with survival words: olá (hello), obrigado (thank you — said by a man; women say obrigada), água (water), comida (food), quanto custa? (how much does it cost?) and onde fica? (where is it?). Add the numbers one to ten and the question words, and you can already handle taxis, cafés and market stalls. Our everyday vocabulary guide gives you sixty of the most useful words in one place.

Spelling that (mostly) helps you

Portuguese spelling follows sound more faithfully than English spelling does. Once you know that ç is always an "s" sound, that nh sounds like the "ny" in "canyon", and that a tilde over a vowel (ã, õ) means "say it through your nose", you can read signs and menus aloud with confidence. Our pronunciation guide walks through every tricky letter.

A realistic study routine

Fifteen minutes a day beats a monthly marathon. Spend five minutes on sounds, five on new words, and five speaking aloud — even to yourself. Use the translator on this site to check sentences about your own life: your family, your job, your town. Personal sentences stick in memory far better than textbook ones, and Brazilians you meet will happily correct you over a coffee.

Above all, speak from the first week. A single bom dia at a bakery in Belo Horizonte will teach you more about Brazilian warmth than a chapter of grammar. Boa sorte — good luck, and welcome to the language.

Pronunciation

Portuguese Pronunciation and the Alphabet: Every Sound Explained

Portuguese uses the same 26-letter alphabet as English, which makes it look deceptively familiar. The spelling rules are actually more consistent than English ones — the challenge is a handful of sounds English does not have. Learn those, and you can pronounce almost any written Portuguese word correctly at first sight.

The letters that behave differently

Most letters match English, but watch these. C before e or i sounds like "s", so cidade (city) is "see-DAH-jee". Ç (c-cedilla) is always "s": açúcar (sugar) is "ah-SOO-kar". G before e or i sounds like the "zh" in "measure": gente (people) is "ZHEN-chee". J is always that same "zh": janeiro is "zhah-NAY-roo". H is silent at the start of words: hoje (today) is simply "OH-zhee".

The famous pairs: lh, nh, ch and rr

Four letter pairs work as single sounds. LH sounds like the "lli" in "million": trabalho (work) is "trah-BAH-lyoo". NH sounds like the "ny" in "canyon": amanhã (tomorrow) ends "mah-NYAH". CH is "sh": chave (key) is "SHAH-vee". And RR — plus a single R at the start of a word — is a throaty "h": carro (car) is "KAH-hoo" and Rio is "HEE-oo". A single r between vowels stays light, which is why caro (expensive) and carro are different words.

The nasal vowels: ão, ãe, õe

The tilde (~) tells you to push the sound through your nose. Ão is the most common ending in the language: pão (bread), não (no), coração (heart). It sounds roughly like "owng" said nasally. Ãe appears in mãe (mother) — "myng", again through the nose — and õe in põe (puts). Nasal endings also hide inside words: any vowel before m or n in the same syllable turns nasal, so bom (good) is "bong" and também (also) is "tam-BENG".

Accent marks and stress

Portuguese stress is predictable: words ending in a, e or o are stressed on the second-to-last syllable; words ending in other letters, on the last. Written accents override everything and also change the sound: á and é are open and bright, ê and ô are closed, and the circumflex in você (you) tells you to close the vowel. The accent can be the only difference between words: avó (grandmother) versus avô (grandfather).

The Brazilian softening of d and t

In most of Brazil, d and t before an "ee" sound turn into "jee" and "chee": dia becomes "JEE-ah", noite (night) becomes "NOY-chee", and cidade ends "DAH-jee". Unstressed final vowels also shrink: leite (milk) sounds like "LAY-chee" with the last vowel almost a whisper. Imitate this and your Portuguese immediately sounds Brazilian rather than European.

Better Results

How to Translate English to Portuguese Accurately

Machine translation between English and Portuguese is strong — the two languages share a Latin-rooted vocabulary — but small habits on your side still make a big difference. These nine tips come from the errors we see most often, including one that no English speaker expects: in Portuguese, even "thank you" has a gender.

1. Obrigado or obrigada? It depends on you

The person saying thanks chooses the ending: men say obrigado, women say obrigada. A translator does not know who is speaking, so it usually defaults to obrigado. If the message is from a woman, change the final o to a — it is the first thing a Brazilian reader will notice.

2. Write complete sentences

Fragments confuse translators because Portuguese verb endings carry information English leaves to context. "Going tomorrow" could be a question, a plan or an order. Write "I am going tomorrow" and the Portuguese comes out clean: Eu vou amanhã.

3. Keep sentences reasonably short

Portuguese handles long sentences well, but a forty-word English sentence with three nested clauses often comes out tangled. Split it into two or three shorter sentences before translating; the result will read like something a person actually wrote.

4. Você or tu? Choose your audience

English has one "you"; Portuguese has several. In most of Brazil, você is the everyday choice for everyone from friends to shopkeepers. Tu survives in parts of the south and north and in Portugal. Translators default to você, which is the safe option for Brazilian readers — leave it unless you know your audience uses tu.

5. Avoid slang and idioms

"It's raining cats and dogs" translated word by word produces very confused Portuguese. Rewrite idioms plainly first — "it is raining heavily" — then translate. The meaning survives; the confusion does not.

6. Beware of false friends

Some words look identical and mean different things. Pretender means "to intend", not "to pretend" (that is fingir). Livraria is a bookshop, not a library (biblioteca). Esquisito means "strange", not "exquisite". When a translation feels odd, this list is the first suspect.

7. Be careful with names and places

Translators sometimes translate names that happen to be ordinary words. If a person's or company's name must stay in English, translate the sentence in two parts and paste the name back in afterwards.

8. Translate paragraph by paragraph

Long pasted documents drift: a name translated one way in paragraph one may change by paragraph five. Work one paragraph at a time, and keep names and technical terms consistent throughout.

9. Back-translate to check

Finally, paste the Portuguese result back into the translator in the Portuguese-to-English direction. If the English that returns matches what you meant, your translation is safe to send. Try it now on the translator — and keep our travel phrases handy if your translation is for a Brazil trip.

Travel

Essential Portuguese Phrases for Travelling in Brazil

Brazil rewards travellers who try even a little Portuguese. In Rio and São Paulo you will find English in hotels and airports, but step into a beach kiosk, a country bus station or a family bakery and Portuguese is the language of the day — and Brazilians respond to learners with legendary warmth. Here are the phrases that matter most, grouped by the moments you will actually use them.

Greetings and courtesy

Open every interaction with the time of day: bom dia (good morning), boa tarde (good afternoon) or boa noite (good evening). Ask como você está? (how are you?) and answer estou bem (I am fine). Thank everyone with obrigado if you are a man, obrigada if you are a woman — Brazilians notice and appreciate the correct form.

Getting around

The traveller's master key is onde fica...? (where is...?) — add any place: onde fica a praia? (where is the beach?). For taxis and ride apps: pare aqui, por favor (please stop here), vire à esquerda (turn left), vire à direita (turn right) and siga em frente (go straight). Settle the price of anything unmetered first with quanto custa? (how much does it cost?).

Food and drink

Brazil eats gloriously. Order um café (a coffee) — it will be small, strong and sweet — or uma água de coco (a coconut water) on the beach. Declare sou vegetariano (I am vegetarian) if needed, ask for menos apimentado, por favor (less spicy, please), and praise the food with está delicioso (it is delicious). The bill is a conta, por favor.

Shopping and bargaining

Quanto custa isto? (how much is this?) opens every purchase. In markets and craft fairs a gentle tem desconto? (is there a discount?) is normal and expected. If you are just browsing, só estou olhando, obrigado (I am just looking, thank you) keeps things friendly.

Emergencies and help

Memorise three lines just in case: socorro! (help!), não estou me sentindo bem (I am not feeling well) and onde fica o hospital? (where is the hospital?). Brazil's police emergency number is 190 and the ambulance service (SAMU) is 192. People in Brazil are quick to help a traveller in trouble — often before you finish the sentence.

Numbers & Counting

Portuguese Numbers 1 to 100: Counting Made Simple

Numbers are among the first things you need in any language. In Brazil you will use them for taxi fares, market prices, hotel rooms, phone numbers and bus times — often several times a day. Portuguese counting is reassuringly regular, and it shares its roots with the numbers in Spanish, French and Italian, so some will already look familiar.

The numbers 1 to 10

Everything starts here: um (one — for feminine words, uma), dois (two), três (three), quatro (four), cinco (five), seis (six), sete (seven), oito (eight), nove (nine) and dez (ten). Say them aloud a few times; vendors will recognise your effort instantly.

The tens: 20 to 100

The tens are tidy: vinte (twenty), trinta (thirty), quarenta (forty), cinquenta (fifty), sessenta (sixty), setenta (seventy), oitenta (eighty), noventa (ninety) and cem (one hundred). Numbers in between join with e (and): twenty-five is vinte e cinco, and ninety-nine is noventa e nove. The teens follow the same habit — sixteen is dezesseis, seventeen dezessete.

Hundreds, thousands and millions

Beyond one hundred, cem becomes cento when more follows: 150 is cento e cinquenta. The other hundreds are single words — duzentos (200), trezentos (300), quinhentos (500). One thousand is mil, and one million is um milhão. A hotel bill of 2,450 reais is dois mil, quatrocentos e cinquenta reais — the same e-joining pattern all the way up.

Numbers in daily life

A few ready-made lines cover most situations. Asking a price: quanto custa? (how much is it?). Saying your age: tenho trinta anos — literally "I have thirty years". Phone numbers are given digit by digit, and Brazilians often say meia ("half") for the digit six on the phone, from the old telephone convention — if you hear it, it means 6, not 0.5. For quantities, the number comes first: dois cafés (two coffees) works in any café in the country.

Practise by counting real things this week — steps, stops, cups of coffee — and test any number sentence in the translator before you use it. Numbers plus the phrases in our shopping guide make market day much easier.

Time & Dates

Days, Months and Telling the Time in Portuguese

"When?" is one of the most practical questions in any language. Whether you are catching a bus, fixing a meeting or planning around Carnaval, you need days, dates and clock times. Portuguese keeps this simple: a small set of words covers almost every plan you will make.

The seven days

The Brazilian week runs: segunda-feira (Monday — literally "second fair"), terça-feira (Tuesday), quarta-feira (Wednesday), quinta-feira (Thursday), sexta-feira (Friday), sábado (Saturday) and domingo (Sunday). In everyday speech the -feira is often dropped: na segunda simply means "on Monday". The numbering is a church-calendar leftover — Sunday was the Lord's day, and Monday counted as the second day.

Today, tomorrow and yesterday

Three words handle most planning: hoje (today), amanhã (tomorrow) and ontem (yesterday). Add amanhã de manhã for "tomorrow morning" — a phrase Brazilians enjoy, since amanhã and manhã sit side by side. "See you tomorrow" is até amanhã, worth using daily.

Parts of the day

The day divides into manhã (morning), tarde (afternoon), noite (night and evening) and madrugada (the small hours after midnight). Greetings follow the same map: bom dia until lunch, boa tarde through the afternoon, boa noite from dusk onwards — used both arriving and leaving.

Months of the year

The twelve months are close cousins of the English ones: janeiro, fevereiro, março, abril, maio, junho, julho, agosto, setembro, outubro, novembro and dezembro. Dates put the day first: "on 10 March" is no dia dez de março. Remember that Brazilian summer runs from December to March — janeiro in Rio is beach season, not winter.

Asking and telling the time

Ask que horas são? (what time is it? — literally "what hours are they?"). One o'clock is singular: é uma hora; everything else is plural: são duas horas, são três horas. Minutes are added with e: 3:20 is três e vinte. Half past uses e meia — 7:30 is sete e meia. Midday is meio-dia and midnight is meia-noite, a tidy pair worth memorising together.

Combine these with the numbers guide and you can read any timetable in the country. For longer sentences, draft them in the translator and check the word order before you travel.

Family

Family Words in Portuguese: Relatives and Relationships

Family is the centre of Brazilian social life — Sunday lunches are sacred, grandmothers are heads of state, and half the adults a child meets are honorary uncles and aunts. Portuguese gives you a clear word for every relationship, and Brazilians soften most of them with affectionate short forms. Learning this vocabulary explains a great deal of what you will hear around any Brazilian table.

Parents and grandparents

Father is pai and mother is mãe; children of all ages call them papai and mamãe, the warm everyday equivalents of "dad" and "mum". Grandfather is avô (pronounced ah-VOH, closed o) and grandmother is avó (ah-VAW, open o) — one small accent, two different people, so listen carefully. The grandchildren are netos, and the word for the whole clan is família.

Brothers and sisters

A brother is irmão and a sister is irmã; together they are irmãos. Portuguese does not split siblings by age the way many Asian languages do — instead, context words do the work: irmão mais velho (older brother) and irmã mais nova (younger sister). The youngest child of the family often carries the nickname caçula for life.

Uncles, aunts and cousins

Every uncle is tio and every aunt is tia — whether they are your father's brother, your mother's sister or simply a close family friend. Children address most friendly adults this way, so a Brazilian playground echoes with tios and tias who share no blood at all. Cousins are primo and prima, and the wider circle of relatives is parentes — a false friend: parentes means relatives, while "parents" are pais.

Children, partners and in-laws

A son is filho and a daughter is filha; a baby is bebê and children in general are crianças. A husband is marido and a wife is esposa; boyfriend and girlfriend are namorado and namorada. Marriage brings the in-laws: sogro (father-in-law), sogra (mother-in-law), genro (son-in-law) and nora (daughter-in-law).

Talking about your family

Two sentence frames cover most introductions. "This is my mother" is esta é a minha mãe. "I have two sisters" is eu tenho duas irmãs. Notice minha (my, for feminine words) and meu (my, for masculine words) — the possessive matches the thing owned, not the owner, a pattern our sentence guide explains in full. For your own introduction, continue to the self-introduction guide.

Family questions are friendly conversation in Brazil, not prying — asking about someone's família shows you care. Practise your answers in the translator, and you will be ready for the warmest small talk in South America.

Speaking

Portuguese Greetings and Goodbyes for Every Occasion

Every conversation in Brazil begins and ends with warmth, and the words are easy to learn. Master this small set of greetings and goodbyes and you will never be stuck at the start or the end of a chat again — in a home, a shop, an office or on the phone.

Olá, oi and the time-of-day greetings

Olá is the textbook hello, but the word you will hear ten times more often is oi — a short, friendly "hi" used everywhere. More formal and always safe are the time-of-day greetings: bom dia (good morning), boa tarde (good afternoon) and boa noite (good evening and good night — the same phrase does both jobs). In shops and restaurants, staff will greet you first; returning the same words with a smile is the whole ritual.

The famous 'tudo bem?' exchange

The standard Brazilian greeting-question is tudo bem? — literally "everything well?" It works like "how are you?" and the standard answer is the same words back: tudo bem — or the fuller tudo bem, e você? (fine, and you?). Alternatives you will hear: como vai? (how goes it?) and the very casual beleza? among friends. Nobody expects a medical report; a cheerful tudo bem keeps the day moving.

Kisses, handshakes and hugs

Greetings in Brazil are physical. Between women, and between women and men, one or two kisses on the cheek are normal in social settings — the number varies by region, and the "kiss" is usually cheek-to-cheek with the sound rather than contact. Men shake hands, often with a pat on the back, and friends of all kinds hug. In business, a handshake is the safe start; follow the other person's lead from there.

On the phone

Brazilians answer the phone with alô? — a word used almost nowhere else in the language. To ask who is speaking: quem está falando?. To say "I will call back", use eu ligo de volta. Calls often close with um abraço (a hug) or um beijo (a kiss) sent down the line to family and close friends.

Welcome and congratulations

Welcome a guest with bem-vindo (to a man) or bem-vinda (to a woman). Congratulate with parabéns — the all-purpose word for birthdays, promotions, new babies and good news of every kind. A birthday gets feliz aniversário, and good luck is boa sorte. Festival wishes get their own guide — see our festivals article.

Saying goodbye — Brazilian style

The everyday goodbye is tchau (from the Italian ciao), often doubled for warmth: tchau, tchau!. Até logo means "see you soon", até amanhã is "see you tomorrow", and the relaxed a gente se fala — "we'll talk" — ends many a phone call. Be warned: a Brazilian goodbye at a party can take twenty minutes of hugs. It is not inefficiency; it is the point.

Try one greeting today — at a shop, with a neighbour, anywhere — and build the habit with the phrasebook. For meeting someone properly for the first time, continue to our self-introduction guide.

Shopping

Shopping and Bargaining in Portuguese: Market Phrases That Work

Brazil's markets — the beachwear stalls of Rio, the sprawling Mercado Municipal in São Paulo, the craft fairs of the Northeast — run on friendly talk. Prices in small shops and market stalls are often the start of a conversation, not the end of one. These phrases will help you shop with confidence and bargain with a smile, which is exactly how it is done locally.

Entering and looking around

Start with bom dia or boa tarde. If you are just browsing, say só estou olhando, obrigado (I am just looking, thank you) — vendors will let you wander in peace. To ask for something specific: você tem...? (do you have...?) followed by the item, or posso ver aquele? (may I see that one?). The word for a shop is loja and for a market, mercado or feira for an open-air street market.

Asking the price

The one question you will use everywhere: quanto custa? (how much does it cost?) — or the shorter quanto é? that Brazilians use constantly. For things sold by weight, ask quanto custa o quilo? (how much per kilo?). Answers come fast, so learn the numbers first. If you did not catch the reply, pode repetir, por favor? (can you repeat, please?) buys you a slower second chance.

Sizes, colours and trying things

Clothing sizes in Brazil use P, M and G — pequeno, médio and grande. Ask tem um tamanho maior? (do you have a bigger size?) or posso provar? (may I try it on?) — the fitting room is the provador. Common colours: vermelho (red), azul (blue), verde (green), preto (black), branco (white) and amarelo (yellow).

Bargaining, politely

In markets and craft fairs — less so in shopping malls with fixed prices — gentle bargaining is expected. The classic opener is tem desconto? (is there a discount?), followed if needed by está um pouco caro (it is a little expensive). Buying several items strengthens your hand: se eu levar dois, você faz um desconto? (if I take two, will you give a discount?). Accept with fechado, eu levo (deal, I'll take it), or decline kindly with vou pensar, obrigado (I'll think about it, thank you).

Paying and finishing

Ask aceita cartão? (do you accept cards?) or aceita Pix? — Pix, Brazil's instant payment system, is accepted almost everywhere, from shopping malls to coconut stalls. Cash is dinheiro, and paying in instalments is parcelado, a deeply Brazilian habit you will see advertised in every shop window. The change you receive back is troco — and if you tell a taxi driver to keep it, the phrase is pode ficar com o troco.

Keep this guide open on market day alongside the phrasebook, and run any tricky sentence through the translator before you set out. Bargaining in Portuguese, even imperfect Portuguese, changes the whole shopping experience.

Emergency

Emergency and Health Phrases in Portuguese

Nobody plans an emergency, but a few memorised lines can make a frightening moment manageable. Brazil has good hospitals in every city and helpful people everywhere; these phrases make sure you can ask for the right help quickly, for yourself or for someone else. Save this page offline before you travel.

Calling for help

The word that cuts through noise is socorro! (help!). Add é uma emergência! (it is an emergency!) to make the urgency plain. Brazil's numbers are worth saving in your phone: 190 for the police, 192 for the ambulance service (SAMU) and 193 for the fire service, which also handles rescues. The calls are free from any phone.

Getting medical help

The essentials: onde fica o hospital? (where is the hospital?), chame um médico (call a doctor) and chame uma ambulância (call an ambulance). The word for a doctor is médico, a hospital is hospital, and the emergency department is the pronto-socorro — the sign to look for when you arrive. Public emergency care in Brazil is free for everyone, including visitors.

Describing what is wrong

Start with não estou me sentindo bem (I am not feeling well). The word dor (pain) combines with body parts: dor de cabeça (headache), dor de barriga (stomach ache), dor de dente (toothache). Other common words: febre (fever), tosse (cough), sangue (blood) and alergia (allergy). "It hurts here" is dói aqui — point as you say it. If you take regular medication, eu tomo este remédio (I take this medicine) plus showing the packet works in any clinic.

At the pharmacy

Medicine is remédio or medicamento, and a pharmacy is a farmácia — Brazilian pharmacies are excellent, staffed late into the night in cities, and pharmacists give good basic advice. Useful lines: você tem este remédio? (do you have this medicine?) and quantas vezes por dia devo tomar? (how many times a day should I take it?).

Police and personal safety

For theft or trouble: chame a polícia (call the police). "I have lost my bag" is perdi minha bolsa, "I need help" is preciso de ajuda, and "I don't understand" — eu não entendo — is the honest phrase that slows any difficult conversation down to a manageable speed. The warning word cuidado! (careful!) is one you will hear shouted kindly at uneven pavements all over Brazil.

Grammar Basics

Portuguese Question Words and Pronouns: Ask Anything

Questions are where a language stops being a phrasebook and becomes a conversation. Portuguese makes asking easy: word order barely changes, and a rising tone at the end of a sentence can turn almost any statement into a question. Learn these few words and you can ask about almost anything in Brazil.

The question words

  • O que? — What? O que é isto? (What is this?)
  • Quem? — Who? Quem é ele? (Who is he?)
  • Onde? — Where? Onde fica a praia? (Where is the beach?)
  • Quando? — When? Quando você volta? (When do you return?)
  • Por quê? — Why? Por que você está aqui? (Why are you here?)
  • Como? — How? Como se diz isto? (How do you say this?)
  • Quanto? — How much / how many? Quanto custa? (How much does it cost?)
  • Qual? — Which? Qual ônibus vai ao centro? (Which bus goes downtown?)

Two small traps

Portuguese splits "why" and "because" by spelling: por que (two words) asks the question, porque (one word) gives the answer — porque sim means "because yes", the classic Brazilian non-answer. And o que versus qual trips up many learners: use qual when choosing from a known set — qual é o seu nome? (what is your name?) is literally "which is your name?", and it is the only natural way to ask.

The pronouns

The personal pronouns are: eu (I), você (you — the everyday Brazilian choice), ele (he), ela (she), nós (we), vocês (you, plural) and eles / elas (they — masculine or mixed / all feminine). You will also meet a gente, literally "the people", which Brazilians use constantly to mean "we": a gente vai amanhã (we are going tomorrow). It takes the same verb form as ele/ela, which makes it a learner's best friend.

Building your first questions

Three patterns cover most of daily life. Pattern one — question word + verb: onde fica o banco? (where is the bank?). Pattern two — the yes/no question, made by tone alone: você fala inglês? (do you speak English?) is just the statement with a lift at the end. Pattern three — the traveller's all-purpose como se diz... em português? (how do you say... in Portuguese?), which turns every Brazilian you meet into your teacher.

Answering questions

Short answers are polite and normal. "Yes" is sim, "no" is não, and "I don't know" is the useful não sei. To agree warmly, Brazilians say claro! (of course!) or com certeza (certainly). For "maybe", the word is talvez.

Culture

Brazilian Festivals and Culture: Words and Wishes

Brazil celebrates like nowhere else on Earth, and its calendar is a festival-lover's dream: the biggest street party on the planet in February, bonfires and square dancing in June, a summertime Christmas, and a New Year spent jumping waves at the beach. Here is what each celebration means — and the exact words to join in.

Carnaval — the greatest show on Earth

Carnaval takes over the country for five days before Lent, usually in February or early March. Rio's samba schools parade through the Sambadrome in a river of feathers and drums, Salvador follows giant sound trucks called trios elétricos, and every town has its blocos — street parties that anyone can join. Key words: fantasia (costume), samba, desfile (parade) and folia (the revelry itself). There is no special greeting — a grin and bom Carnaval! will do.

Festa Junina — June's bonfires and square dances

The Festa Junina honours Saints Anthony, John and Peter with bonfires (fogueiras), checked shirts, straw hats and the quadrilha, a choreographed square dance led by a caller. The food is the real star: canjica (sweet corn pudding), pamonha (corn tamales), roasted peanuts and spiced hot wine. In the Northeast, São João in Campina Grande and Caruaru rivals Carnaval itself. Wish people a feliz São João! in late June.

Natal — a summer Christmas

Christmas in Brazil falls in high summer, so Natal means evening masses, family dinners served late on 24 December, and fireworks at midnight. The table holds roast turkey or chester, rabanada (Brazilian French toast dusted with cinnamon) and panetone, the Italian fruit bread Brazil has adopted as its own. Father Christmas is Papai Noel. The greeting is feliz Natal! — send it any time in December.

Réveillon — New Year at the beach

Brazil's New Year, Réveillon, is celebrated in white clothes for peace and luck, ideally on a beach. In Rio, millions gather on Copacabana for fireworks; tradition says jumping seven waves and making a wish at midnight brings fortune, and offerings of flowers are made to Iemanjá, queen of the sea. The greeting is feliz ano novo!, and the first of January is a day of rest and leftover feasting.

Independence Day and everyday celebrations

On 7 September, Dia da Independência brings parades and a sea of green and yellow. Around the year you will also meet birthday parties where the guest of honour is sung parabéns pra você — the Brazilian birthday song — and football matches that function as festivals in their own right. When in doubt, the all-purpose celebration words are parabéns! (congratulations) and saúde! (cheers — literally "health!").

How to Introduce Yourself in Portuguese

Introductions are the first real conversation you will have in any language, and in Brazil they matter: people genuinely want to know who you are, where you come from and what brings you to their country. This guide builds your introduction piece by piece, so by the end you can deliver the whole thing without notes.

Your name

Start with meu nome é... (my name is...) or the shorter, very common eu sou... (I am...). To ask back: qual é o seu nome? (what is your name?) — remember from our questions guide that Portuguese uses qual, "which", for names. Add prazer em conhecê-lo (pleased to meet you) and the formalities are complete.

Where you are from

The pattern is eu sou de... (I am from...) plus your city or country: eu sou de Londres, eu sou do Paquistão. Countries take the article do/da in Portuguese — dos Estados Unidos, da Inglaterra — while most cities stand alone. To say you live somewhere now: eu moro em São Paulo (I live in São Paulo). Expect the follow-up é a sua primeira vez no Brasil? (is it your first time in Brazil?) — answer sim, é a primeira vez or não, já vim antes (no, I have been before).

Age, family and the personal basics

Age uses the verb "to have": eu tenho trinta anos (I am thirty — literally "I have thirty years"). Family lines follow the same frame: eu sou casado (I am married, man) or casada (woman), eu tenho dois filhos (I have two children). Our family guide has every relative you might need to mention.

Work and studies

Say your job with eu trabalho com... (I work with/in...) — eu trabalho com computadores — or name the profession: sou professor (I am a teacher), sou engenheiro (engineer), sou estudante (student). Note that Portuguese drops the word for "a" before professions: it is simply sou professor, not "sou um professor". If you are learning the language, saying so wins friends instantly: estou aprendendo português (I am learning Portuguese).

A full example

Put together, a complete introduction sounds like this: Olá! Meu nome é Ahmed. Eu sou do Paquistão, mas agora moro em Dubai. Tenho trinta e dois anos, sou casado e tenho uma filha. Eu trabalho com tecnologia e estou aprendendo português porque adoro o Brasil. Prazer em conhecê-lo! — "Hello! My name is Ahmed. I am from Pakistan, but now I live in Dubai. I am thirty-two, married, and I have a daughter. I work in technology and I am learning Portuguese because I love Brazil. Pleased to meet you!"

Write your own version, run it through the translator to check it, then say it aloud ten times. Finish with the phrases from our greetings guide and your first Brazilian conversation is ready before you land.

Food & Drink

Portuguese for Food Lovers: Eating Well in Brazil

Brazil is one of the world's great food countries, and eating well there is a language skill as much as an appetite. This guide walks the classic dishes, explains how Brazilian restaurants work, and gives you the phrases to order confidently from a beach kiosk to a São Paulo steakhouse.

The everyday plate: arroz e feijão

The backbone of Brazilian eating is arroz e feijão — rice and beans — served at lunch in homes and restaurants across the country, usually with a protein, a salad and farofa, toasted cassava flour sprinkled over everything. The set lunch built on it is the prato feito, often shortened to PF: Brazil's best-value meal. The weekday rhythm matters too: Wednesday and Saturday are traditional feijoada days in many restaurants.

Feijoada — the national dish

Feijoada is a slow black-bean stew cooked with pork and beef, served with rice, shredded kale (couve), orange slices, farofa and hot sauce. It began as humble food and became the country's Saturday celebration — a meal designed for long tables and longer afternoons. Order a feijoada completa for the full works, and pace yourself: nobody finishes a feijoada quickly, and nobody is meant to.

Snacks and sweets: pão de queijo and brigadeiro

Pão de queijo — warm, chewy cheese breads made with cassava flour — are Minas Gerais' gift to the world and the natural partner of a coffee. Brazil's beloved party sweet is the brigadeiro, a fudge-like ball of condensed milk and chocolate rolled in sprinkles; its spoonable party rival is the beijinho, the coconut version. Street snacks to know: coxinha (a teardrop of shredded chicken in dough), pastel (a crisp fried pastry, best at street fairs) and açaí, the Amazonian berry served thick and cold.

Churrasco — the barbecue ritual

A churrascaria is a steakhouse running on the rodízio system: waiters circulate with skewers of grilled meats — the prized cut is picanha — and slice them onto your plate until you surrender by turning your table card to red. Start at the salad bar if you like, but veterans warn it is a trap for the unwary appetite. Ask for your meat mal passada (rare), ao ponto (medium) or bem passada (well done).

Ordering and paying

To order: eu queria... (I would like...) is the polite all-rounder, and para mim, um suco de laranja (for me, an orange juice) shows the pattern. Juice culture is huge — try suco de maracujá (passion fruit). Vegetarians say sou vegetariano; for allergies, tenho alergia a amendoim (I am allergic to peanuts). Finish with a conta, por favor (the bill, please) — service of around 10% is usually already included as the taxa de serviço.

How Portuguese Sentences Are Built: Word Order and Verb Endings

Every language assembles sentences its own way, and Portuguese is kind to English speakers: the basic order is the same as English, and the differences are regular patterns rather than exceptions. Understand the five ideas in this guide and Portuguese sentences will start assembling themselves in your head.

Word order: subject, verb, object

The standard pattern matches English exactly. "The woman reads the book" is a mulher lê o livro — subject, verb, object, in the same order. Adjectives break the pattern in one predictable way: they usually follow the noun. "A big house" is uma casa grande ("a house big") and "a red car" is um carro vermelho. A small group of common adjectives — bom (good), grande in the sense of "great", novo — can come first and slightly change their flavour, but the after-the-noun default is always safe.

Every noun has a gender

Portuguese nouns are masculine or feminine, and the little words around them must agree. Words ending in -o are usually masculine (o livro, the book) and words ending in -a usually feminine (a casa, the house). "The" is o or a, "a" is um or uma, and adjectives match too: o carro novo but a casa nova. There are exceptions — o dia (the day) is masculine — but the -o/-a rule covers the great majority, and Brazilians forgive gender slips without blinking.

Verbs change for each person

Where English barely changes its verbs, Portuguese dresses them for every person. Most verbs end in -ar, -er or -ir, and each family has its endings. Take falar (to speak): eu falo (I speak), você fala (you speak), nós falamos (we speak), eles falam (they speak). The pattern repeats for thousands of verbs, which is why Brazilians often drop the pronoun entirely — the ending already says who. The two verbs you need first are gloriously irregular: ser (to be — identity) and estar (to be — state). "I am Brazilian" (identity) is eu sou brasileiro; "I am tired" (state) is eu estou cansado.

Saying no, and asking questions

Negation is beautifully simple: put não before the verb. "I speak" — eu falo; "I don't speak" — eu não falo. Double negatives are normal and correct: não sei nada (I don't know anything — literally "I don't know nothing"). Questions need no rearrangement at all: say the statement with a rising voice — você fala inglês? — or start with a question word from our questions guide.

Time without tenses

Portuguese does have past and future verb forms, but everyday speech leans on helpful shortcuts. The near future is usually vou + verb: vou viajar amanhã (I am going to travel tomorrow) — the same shape as English "going to". The recent past often uses acabei de: acabei de chegar (I have just arrived). Learn these two frames and you can talk about most of your day with present-tense verbs alone.

How do I pronounce the Portuguese translation?

The phrasebook gives a "Say it" pronunciation for every phrase, and the pronunciation guide explains every tricky sound — the nasal ão, the ç, lh and nh, and the throaty rr — with examples.

About Translate English to Portuguese

Translate English to Portuguese is a free language website built for one simple job: helping English speakers understand, speak and learn Brazilian Portuguese. It combines three tools in one place — a fast online translator, a hand-checked phrasebook of the phrases travellers and learners actually use, and a library of sixteen original guides that explain the language in plain English.

The site focuses on Brazilian Portuguese because Brazil is home to more than 210 million speakers — over four-fifths of the Portuguese-speaking world. Everything here, from the pronunciation notes to the festival calendar, reflects how Portuguese is really spoken in Brazil today.
